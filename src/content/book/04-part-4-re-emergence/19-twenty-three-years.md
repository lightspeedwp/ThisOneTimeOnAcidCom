LightSpeedDevelopment — LSD — was founded in January 2003. I was twenty-two years old. The acronym was intentional. The company wasn't born from a business plan; it was born from a fundamental need for autonomy. The same brain that rejected boredom in school rejected being told what to do at work. The solution was to build something of my own.

You never know if you're ready to start a business, but I knew that I didn't want to work at City Varsity for another year. I've always been entrepreneurial, but I really didn't like working for an employer. I have my own ideas and things that drive me. I have my own way — when you work for somebody else they tell you what to do and I would prefer to decide what I do. Throughout my whole life, I've designed my life the way I want to live it.

The company is the longest project I have ever worked on — longer than any festival circuit, any cycling route, any relationship. Twenty-three years is more than half my life. The lessons compound over decades.

---

The early years were chaotic in the way all startups are chaotic. I was twenty-two, working from a spare bedroom in Cape Town, doing IT support for anyone who would pay. The name was deliberately chosen — LightSpeedDevelopment. LSD. That was intentional.

Hiring the first employee in 2005 was the biggest psychological adjustment of the journey. The company was no longer just me. I was responsible for someone else's livelihood, and that weight changed how I thought about the business overnight.

Then came BarCamp Cape Town in 2006 — the event that changed everything. Dave Duarte gave an impassioned talk about this open-source CMS called WordPress and why it was going to change the web. Something clicked. Not just the technology — the philosophy. Open source. Community-driven. Transparent. Accessible. These were the same values my parents had instilled. That single unconference redirected LightSpeed from IT support to WordPress web development. Everything that followed traces back to that room.

Warwick Booth joined in December 2006. He has been by my side for nearly two decades. When I think about what makes LightSpeed work, Warwick is the first name that comes to mind. He understood the vision from day one.

---

The team grew slowly, each person adding something essential. Chris Vancoillie brought systems administration strength in 2009. Barbara joined in 2010 and became a partner soon after — she took on the operations, the finances, the human management skills that my ADHD brain struggles with. Without Barbara, LightSpeed would be a very different company. She brought the structure I need but cannot create alone. Our partnership — personal and professional — is one of the most important relationships of my life.

What people don't always know is that I wasn't always solo. Between 2008 and 2011, I worked at Media24 as a Scrum Master and Business Analyst. Three years of learning process, team management, and corporate-scale thinking that I brought straight back to LightSpeed. It gave me tools — sprint planning, retrospectives, structured feedback — that I still use today, just in my own way.

José Abreu first joined in 2017, left after two years, and returned in September 2025. Justin Abrahamse came back after years away. Lourens Visser joined in 2021 — the same Lourens who taught me to swim properly. The fact that people choose to return tells you something about the culture. By 2023, Tibi Buzdugan and Zared Rogers had joined, and the team was expanding into its strongest configuration yet.

In 2025, three interns joined — Hugo Gravito, Brandon Marshall, and Seren van der Merwe — selected from 450 LinkedIn applicants. José returned the same month. Thirteen people. The most optimal team in twenty-three years.

— BARCAMP CAPE TOWN 2006: THE STORY —

BarCamp was an unconference — no preset agenda, no keynote speakers, no hierarchy. Attendees showed up and created the programme on the morning of the event. Topics were written on Post-it notes and arranged on a whiteboard. If you wanted to talk about something, you claimed a slot.

Dave Duarte was there. He gave an impassioned talk about this open-source CMS called WordPress and why it was going to change the web. I was running an IT support company and dabbling in web design. Something clicked. Not just the technology — the philosophy. Open source. Community-driven. Transparent. Accessible. These were the same values my parents had instilled.

The people at that BarCamp didn't just introduce me to WordPress — they introduced me to a way of working and building that would define the next two decades. Jeremy Thurgood, Jonathan Sobel, and dozens of others who became part of the extended LightSpeed network. Twenty-nine people at that event impacted my career in ways I'm still discovering.

— WHAT WE BUILD —

The LSX Design System is the thing I'm most proud of technically. It started as a Figma file and grew into a comprehensive component library, typography scale, colour system, and spacing framework that bridges design and development. I spoke about it at WordCamp Europe 2025 in Basel, and standing on that stage explaining something I'd built from scratch was one of the highlights of my career.

Tour Operator came from a practical need. Our South African client base included travel companies, and there was nothing in the WordPress ecosystem that handled bookings, itineraries, and third-party travel system integration the way they needed. So we built it. That's always been the pattern at LightSpeed — a client needs something, it doesn't exist, so we make it open source and give it to the community.

Open source has been central to LightSpeed's identity since the WordPress pivot. We contribute through code, speaking, organising, and mentoring. I organised WordCamp Cape Town in 2011 and 2012. I've been on the WordPress Theme Review Team. Every time we give something back, the community gives back tenfold. That's not philosophy — that's twenty-three years of proof.

— THE AI SHIFT —

I'll be honest: when AI started becoming practical in 2024, I had a moment of genuine disorientation. After two decades of building things by hand — every line of CSS, every WordPress hook, every Figma component — suddenly there was a tool that could generate in minutes what used to take hours.

The first time I paired with GitHub Copilot on a real project, I sat back in my chair and said something that I won't repeat here. It wasn't fear — it was the realisation that the game had changed overnight, and I needed to change with it. So I dove in. Copilot for code. ChatGPT for research and brainstorming. Claude for deep analysis and long-form thinking. Model Context Protocol for connecting AI to our development tools.

Now I'm mentoring the entire team into this new way of working. The team is levelling up week on week at a pace I've never seen in my career. AI amplifies what's already there. If the foundation is solid — curiosity, work ethic, willingness to learn — it turns good developers into exceptional ones. This portfolio website itself was built using AI-assisted workflows. The future isn't AI replacing developers. It's developers who use AI replacing developers who don't.

— WHAT TWENTY-THREE YEARS TEACHES YOU —

Freedom requires structure. I learned that the hard way — the year I gave the team total flexibility without systems in place, and we missed every deadline for three months straight. The ADHD brain needs guardrails. So I built meticulous systems — GitHub workflows, daily planning templates, curriculum structures — and now the structure creates the freedom, not the other way around.

The right people change everything. Warwick joining in 2006 changed the trajectory. Barbara in 2010 changed the trajectory again. Every right hire shifted the direction. The wrong hires taught equally valuable and equally expensive lessons. Hiring is the single most impactful thing a founder does, and I got it wrong more times than I care to admit before I got it right.

The business feeds the art, and the art feeds the business. The same brain that builds WordPress design systems is the brain that designs neon faces at 3am on a dancefloor. LightSpeed has run profitably for twenty-three years because the entrepreneurial drive and the creative drive are the same thing — not a balance to be managed, but a single engine with two outputs.

Autonomy is non-negotiable. The freedom given to the LightSpeed team mirrors the freedom I demand for myself. Happy team, better work, better company. The company is the container that makes the rest possible — the Berlin summers, the Thailand training, the festival circuits, the art. LightSpeed is the financial engine that funds a life lived in full colour. And it works because it was never just a business. It was always the longest creative project of a restless life.
