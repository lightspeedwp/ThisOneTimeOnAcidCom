It started with a yellow suit from a charity shop and the discovery that a dancefloor is a stage if you want it to be. The evolution happened in stages, and each one taught me something about visibility, identity, and the courage to be absurd on purpose.

The yellow suit earned me the nickname the Chicken Man. This was the beginning of standing out deliberately — of treating the dancefloor as a performance space. Then the red suit — escalation. If one loud suit works, go louder.

Then the white and black cow suit. The pivot from dressing up to becoming a character. No longer just a guy in a bold outfit — now I had a persona. The brown and beige cow suit cemented it: I became the Cow Man, for years, at every festival. People STILL ask if I'm the guy who used to dress as the cow man.

---

In the 2000s I was going to two to four festivals a month in Cape Town. Every single Alien Safari. Every single Vortex. This wasn't casual attendance. This was a way of life. The dancefloor was classroom, gallery, church, and community centre.

The psytrance buddies became a staple in life. The scene was small enough to know everyone but big enough to always surprise you. The South African outdoor trance scene in the early 2000s was something magical — wild landscapes, clear stars, proper sound systems, and a community that genuinely cared about each other.

I became very well known at parties because of the unique outfits. But what I didn't understand yet was that the costumes were preparation. Twenty years of standing out on purpose, of being the most visible person on every dancefloor, of reading energy and crowd dynamics — all of it was unconscious training for the moment I'd finally pick up a paintbrush.

---

The costume timeline tells the story of an identity being built without the owner knowing it.

1999–2000: The yellow suit. Chicken Man era. Vortex festivals in the Western Cape mountains, Alien Safari in the Boland hills. A twenty-year-old in a charity-shop suit dancing like nobody was watching — except everyone was. That was the point.

2001–2003: The red suit. If one loud suit works, try louder. The red suit said: I am here, I am not apologising, and I will dance for twelve hours straight. People started recognising me between festivals. "You're the suit guy." Identity was forming — though I couldn't have named it then.

2003–2006: The white and black cow suit. This wasn't dressing up any more. This was character work. The cow suit had a narrative, a personality, a following. People didn't just notice me; they told stories about me to friends who hadn't been there. That's when I learned the difference between being seen and being remembered.

2006–2010: The brown and beige cow suit. The Cow Man identity fully cemented. At every festival, someone would shout across the dancefloor: "It's the Cow Man!" A decade later, people still approach me: "Are you the guy who used to dress as a cow?" The recognition moments stacked into something bigger than a costume. They became proof that standing out is a practice, not a talent.

2010–2018: The evolution beyond a single character. Onesies, UV-reactive clothing, painted designs on fabric. The costumes were migrating closer to the body, closer to the skin, closer to what was coming. I just didn't know it yet.

---

The insight only landed years later: every costume was a rehearsal.

Every time I put on a loud suit and walked onto a dancefloor, I was practising the willingness to be seen. Not just seen but stared at, pointed at, photographed, approached by strangers. That kind of nerve builds slowly, one festival at a time, one ridiculous outfit at a time. By the time I picked up UV paint in 2019, I had twenty years of practice being the centre of attention. The transition from wearing costumes to painting them onto other people was seamless.

The suits and onesies were armour — external identity layered on top of the person. UV paint on skin is the opposite. It transforms the person themselves. No hiding behind fabric. The art is on you, part of you, inseparable from you.

Twenty years of costumes taught me one thing that made me a better makeup artist: standing out is a skill, and once you've mastered it for yourself, you can teach others to do it. That is what I do now. I don't just paint faces — I give people permission to be the most visible person on the dancefloor. I know what that feels like because I have been doing it since 1999.
