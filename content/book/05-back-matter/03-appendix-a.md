There is an alternative version of this book living in my head. Instead of a chronological memoir organised by life phases, it's structured entirely through the visual and identity evolution: early days of dancing, the yellow suit, the red suit, the cow suit, the rave outfits, and finally the UV paint. "Dance Like No One's Watching" is the working title.

Whether it becomes a standalone book or merges into this one remains to be decided. The material overlaps — the costume evolution chapter already reads like a condensed version. But there is something powerful about tracing the journey purely through the lens of visibility: from a kid who was bullied for being small to an artist who makes strangers glow.

The thesis would be: standing out is a skill, not a talent. It builds through practice, one wild outfit at a time, one dancefloor at a time, until the day you realise that you are no longer performing visibility — you are it. The costume became the identity. The identity became the art.

---

The proposed structure traces five visual eras: The Suit Years (1999–2003). The Character Years (2003–2010). The Transition Years (2010–2018). The UV Revelation (2019–present). And a speculative final chapter on where the art goes next — full-body UV installations, collaborative live painting, video documentation, and the intersection of AI-generated visual design with handmade UV art.

Each era maps to a psychological shift. The suits were about courage. The characters were about identity. The transition was about searching. The UV paint was about finding. The next phase — whatever it is — will be about deepening. The visual timeline is really a map of self-discovery, charted in neon.

If this book is the chronological memoir, Dance Like No One's Watching is the artistic memoir. Same life, different lens. Same dancefloor, different spotlight. Both are true. Both are necessary. The question is whether they're better together or apart.
