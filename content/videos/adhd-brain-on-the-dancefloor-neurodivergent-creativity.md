## The Neurology Behind the Art

For most of my life, I tried to hide my ADHD. The impulsivity, the inability to sit still, the hyperfocus that makes you lose track of time, the sensory overload that makes normal environments unbearable.

Then I discovered the dancefloor. And everything changed.

This video is an honest exploration of how ADHD neurology doesn't just shape my art — it makes my art possible.

### The ADHD Traits That Are Actually Superpowers

**Hyperfocus:** The ability to lock into a task so completely that the rest of the world disappears. Normal people call this "flow state." For ADHD brains, it's Tuesday.

When I'm painting a face at 2am on a dancefloor, I can hyperfocus for hours. Twelve faces in a row without a break. The music fades into background noise. The crowd disappears. There's only the canvas, the brush, and the pattern emerging.

**Sensory Processing:** ADHD brains process sensory input differently. Loud music, flashing lights, crowded spaces — the things that overwhelm neurotypical people are the things that regulate me.

The dancefloor isn't overstimulating for me. It's **optimal stimulation.** The bass regulates my nervous system. The UV lights focus my attention. The crowd provides just enough chaos to keep my brain engaged.

**Pattern Recognition:** ADHD brains are wired to recognize patterns. It's why we're good at debugging code, solving puzzles, and creating symmetrical designs freehand.

Sacred geometry, mandalas, fractals — these aren't difficult for me to paint. My brain sees the patterns instantly. The challenge is slowing down enough to execute them with precision.

**Impulsivity:** The trait that causes problems in daily life (interrupting conversations, making rash decisions, starting projects without finishing them) becomes an asset in creative work.

Impulsivity means I don't overthink. I don't second-guess. I trust my hands and let the design flow. The best work happens when I stop planning and just paint.

### The Dancefloor as Ideal Environment

Here's what most people don't understand about ADHD: **we don't struggle with attention. We struggle with regulating attention in boring environments.**

Give an ADHD brain a stimulating, high-energy, multi-sensory environment — like a psytrance dancefloor at 3am — and we thrive.

**Why the dancefloor works:**
- Constant auditory stimulation (driving basslines, hypnotic loops)
- Constant visual stimulation (UV lights, projections, moving bodies)
- Constant kinesthetic stimulation (standing, moving, painting)
- No requirement to sit still or mask
- Social interaction on my terms (people come to me, I control the pace)
- Clear, immediate feedback (finished face = visible result)

This isn't just a good environment for ADHD. It's the **ideal** environment.

### The Challenges

I'm not romanticizing ADHD. The dancefloor works because it's highly structured novelty. But the rest of life is hard.

**Daily struggles:**
- Admin tasks (invoices, emails, scheduling) take 10x longer than they should
- Rejection-sensitive dysphoria makes criticism feel like personal attacks
- Time blindness means I'm either two hours early or 30 minutes late
- Executive dysfunction makes starting tasks nearly impossible

The difference: on the dancefloor, I don't need executive function. The environment provides the structure. The music provides the rhythm. The queue of people provides the urgency.

### Medication and Self-Awareness

I take ADHD medication. It helps with the admin tasks, the time management, the ability to function in neurotypical spaces.

But I don't take medication before painting. The hyperfocus, the impulsivity, the pattern recognition — these are the traits that make the art work. I don't want to medicate them away.

Self-awareness is the key. Know when you need support and when you need to lean into your neurology.

> The ADHD brain that struggles with daily life thrives in festival conditions. Loud music, dark rooms, crowded spaces, constant movement — this is where my neurology performs best.

This video is for every neurodivergent artist who's been told they're "too much" or "not enough." You're not broken. You just haven't found your dancefloor yet.
