# When the dancefloors went dark

March 2020. South Africa went into hard lockdown. The festivals vanished overnight. The dancefloors went dark. For a brain wired for sensory richness and community connection, the silence was deafening.

## What disappeared

Try to explain to a neurotypical person what losing the dancefloor means to an ADHD brain. It’s not losing a hobby. It’s losing your primary regulation system. The sensory richness that keeps the brain calm. The community that provides external structure. The physical endurance of 12-hour dances that anchors the mind.

No festivals. No Alien Safari. No Vortex. No Berlin open-airs. No Thailand parties. The entire social infrastructure that I’d built over twenty years — gone, indefinitely.

## What remained

The bicycle was still there. When the world went quiet, the roads went empty, and the mountain passes opened up in a way they hadn’t before. Cycling became the only sensory outlet — the wind, the exertion, the rhythm of pedalling, the landscape scrolling past. Not a dancefloor, but the closest available approximation.

LightSpeed was still there. The business didn’t stop — if anything, demand for web development increased as everyone moved online. Remote-first since 2003, we were accidentally pandemic-proof. The team adapted without drama because remote work wasn’t a new concept. It was just Tuesday.

The cats were still there. Timmy, Wendy, Jimmy, Bean. The garden still needed tending. The soil still needed feeding. The rhythm of cultivation doesn’t respect pandemics. The worm tea still needed brewing every two weeks.

## The question

COVID forced a question I hadn’t asked in years: who am I without the dancefloor?

The answer took time. I was the cyclist. The gardener. The business owner. The cat person. The builder. The person who taught himself Windows from twelve stiffy discs and built everything that followed from that same stubbornness.

The dancefloor had given me everything, but it wasn’t everything I was. The other threads — the cycling, the cultivation, the code — held firm when the music stopped.

## The return

When festivals came back, slowly and tentatively, the return was electric. The first outdoor party after months of silence was overwhelming — the bass in the chest, the UV lights, the faces of friends not seen in a year. It felt like coming home after a long, quiet exile.

But I came back different. More grateful. More aware that the things we take for granted can vanish overnight. And with a quiet certainty that when the world opens again, you come back as someone who has survived the silence and found that the beat was inside you all along.
