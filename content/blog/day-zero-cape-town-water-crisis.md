# Day Zero: when Cape Town almost ran out of water

In 2018, Cape Town almost became the first major city in the modern world to run out of water. The media called it Day Zero — the projected date when the taps would literally be turned off and residents would queue at municipal collection points for 25 litres per person per day.

Day Zero never arrived. The city survived through aggressive water restrictions, behavioural change, and eventual rain. But the crisis fundamentally changed how we live.

## The restriction reality

At the height of the crisis, every Cape Town household was limited to 50 litres per person per day. To put that in perspective: a typical shower uses 60–80 litres. A single toilet flush uses 9 litres. You learned to shower in 90 seconds, to flush only when necessary, to reuse grey water for the garden.

I was already an outdoor grower by this point — the garden that would eventually become Six Cats was well established. Watching the dam levels drop below 20% and knowing that my plants needed water forced a reckoning: either find a sustainable water source or stop growing.

## The rainwater solution

We installed 13,500 litres of rainwater storage with triple filtration — sediment, carbon, and UV stages. Every drop that touches our plants comes from the sky. No chlorine, no fluoride, no municipal dependency.

The system wasn’t cheap, and installing it during a water crisis meant competing with every other Cape Town resident who had the same idea. But it was one of the best decisions I’ve ever made. Cape Town’s water situation has stabilised since then, but the habit of treating every drop as precious has stayed.

## What Day Zero taught me

Day Zero taught me that sustainability isn’t a marketing term — it’s a survival strategy. When your city nearly runs dry, you stop treating resources as infinite. You start building closed-loop systems: Bokashi composting feeds the worm farm, the worm farm feeds the soil, rainwater feeds the plants, the plants feed the cycle.

The same philosophy now underpins everything at Six Cats. Glass packaging returned and reused. Worm compost tea brewed every two weeks. Companion planting instead of chemical pest control. Nothing synthetic. Nothing wasted.

## The broader lesson

Day Zero also taught me that community matters during crisis. Neighbours shared water-saving tips. Restaurants displayed their usage. The city pulled together in a way that felt familiar to anyone who has been part of a festival community — collective responsibility, shared sacrifice, genuine care for each other.

Cape Town survived. The taps stayed on. But the memory of how close we came stays with every Cape Town resident. It’s why I take water personally, not philosophically.
