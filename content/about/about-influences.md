# The forces that forged the vision

Artists, movements, festivals, and cultural currents that shaped every neon brushstroke

No artist creates in a vacuum. This is the map of influences — the people, places, sounds, and moments that converged to create the aesthetic, philosophy, and techniques behind the UV work you see today.

## Artistic influences

The visual artists, musicians, and creators whose work cracked open new ways of seeing and creating.

### Android Jones

VISIONARY ARTIST

Pioneer of digital psychedelic art and live projection mapping

Android Jones showed me that psychedelic geometry isn't random chaos — it's structured complexity with intention. His work taught me how sacred geometry, fractal patterns, and vibrant color theory could converge into coherent visual language. Watching his live projection sets at festivals was like seeing the interior world of expanded consciousness mapped onto external reality. That translation — inner vision to outer expression — became foundational to how I approach UV face painting.

Key lessons:

- Sacred geometry as visual syntax
- Fractal complexity with intentional structure
- Color theory in psychedelic contexts
- Live performance as transformative art

### Alex Grey

VISIONARY ARTIST

Master of anatomical mysticism and energetic body visualization

Alex Grey's work bridges the physical and the energetic. His anatomical paintings don't just show the body — they reveal the layers of energy, consciousness, and interconnection beneath the skin. That concept — making the invisible visible — is exactly what UV face painting does under blacklight. The neon isn't decoration. It's revealing the energy field that's always there. Grey's work validated that artistic practice can be spiritual practice.

Key lessons:

- The body as sacred architecture
- Making invisible energy visible
- Art as spiritual practice
- Anatomical precision meets mystical vision

### Robert Venosa

VISIONARY ARTIST

Biomechanical mysticism and otherworldly landscapes

Venosa's biomechanical landscapes feel both organic and technological, familiar and alien. His work taught me that surrealism doesn't have to abandon structure. You can bend reality while maintaining technical mastery. His influence shows up in how I blend geometric precision with organic flow in UV designs — the sharp lines of sacred geometry softening into the curves of natural forms.

Key lessons:

- Biomechanical fusion aesthetics
- Technical precision in surreal contexts
- Balancing structure and flow
- Otherworldly beauty grounded in craft

### Bassnectar

ELECTRONIC MUSIC PRODUCER

Bass music as emotional architecture and festival culture icon

Bassnectar's production philosophy — maximum emotional impact through bass frequencies and layered textures — directly influenced how I think about visual composition. His sets taught me how to build tension, create peaks, and allow release. I apply that same rhythm to face painting: slow build, intricate details, explosive color payoff. His music was the soundtrack to countless painting sessions and festival moments.

Key lessons:

- Emotional architecture through layering
- Building tension and release
- Bass frequencies as physical sensation
- Community-building through art

### Shpongle

PSYCHEDELIC MUSIC DUO

World fusion psychedelia and audio-visual synesthesia

Simon Posford and Raja Ram created a sonic universe that feels visual. Listening to Shpongle is like watching colors dance. Their fusion of world music, electronic production, and psychedelic sensibility taught me that cultural hybridity isn't appropriation when approached with reverence and research. The way they weave Middle Eastern scales, Indian instrumentation, and cutting-edge synthesis mirrors how I blend geometric traditions from multiple cultures in UV work.

Key lessons:

- Audio-visual synesthesia
- Respectful cultural fusion
- Organic meets electronic
- Music as transportive experience

### Salvador Dalí

SURREALIST PAINTER

Technical mastery in service of impossible visions

Dalí proved that you can paint dreams with classical technique. His surrealism isn't sloppy abstraction — it's precise rendering of impossible realities. That lesson stuck: master the fundamentals so you can break them intentionally. My geometric face painting requires technical precision (steady hands, clean lines, color theory) even when the final result looks otherworldly. Dalí's audacity to take surrealism seriously as craft inspired that approach.

Key lessons:

- Technical mastery enables creative freedom
- Precision in impossible contexts
- Surrealism as serious craft
- Dreamscapes require structure

## Cultural movements and scenes

The subcultures, communities, and collective energies that provided context and belonging.

### Psytrance culture

THE FOUNDATIONAL COMMUNITY AND AESTHETIC HOME

Psytrance wasn't just the music I listened to — it became the ecosystem I inhabited. The global psytrance community taught me that art, spirituality, and celebration could be inseparable. Festival culture introduced me to UV reactive everything: decor, clothing, body paint. The visual language of psytrance (fractals, mandalas, alien landscapes, DMT-inspired geometry) became my visual vocabulary. This culture values transformation, consciousness exploration, and aesthetic experimentation — all values that now define my work.

### Berlin techno underground

WHERE HEDONISM MEETS RITUAL AND THE DANCEFLOOR BECOMES CEREMONY

Berlin's techno scene taught me that clubs are more than entertainment venues — they're secular temples. The commitment to multi-day sessions, the refusal of photography inside sacred spaces like Berghain, the understanding that dancing can be meditative practice — these values reshaped how I see UV face painting. Painting someone's face before they enter Griessmuehle or Sisyphos isn't vanity. It's ritual preparation. Berlin taught me to take nightlife seriously as transformative practice.

### Burning Man ethos

RADICAL SELF-EXPRESSION, GIFT ECONOMY, AND LEAVE NO TRACE PRINCIPLES

Though I haven't attended Burning Man itself, the ripple effects of its 10 Principles shaped festival culture globally. Radical self-expression gave permission to be weird. Gifting without expectation introduced me to alternative economic models. Radical inclusion created spaces where everyone belongs. These aren't just festival rules — they're philosophical frameworks that inform how I approach collaboration, teaching, and community building in the UV art world.

### South African festival culture

RAW, AUTHENTIC, OUTDOOR TRANSFORMATION IN THE AFRICAN LANDSCAPE

South African festivals like Origin, Vortex, and Forest Trance have a unique character — they blend global psytrance culture with African landscapes, local tribes, and a rawness you don't find in European festivals. Outdoor sound systems in the Drakensberg mountains. Sunrise sets over the Karoo desert. This culture taught me that festivals don't need massive production budgets to create transformation. The land itself is the stage. That authenticity shows up in my work: less polish, more presence.

### Van life and nomadic creatives

PROOF THAT CREATIVE FREEDOM AND MOBILITY ARE COMPATIBLE

The global community of van dwellers, bicycle tourers, and digital nomads showed me that location independence isn't just for tech workers. Artists can be nomadic too. Seeing festival painters traveling Europe in converted vans, musicians touring on bicycles with portable gear, photographers documenting journeys across continents — it normalized the lifestyle I now live. You don't need a studio to create. You need skills, mobility, and courage.

### Open source ethos

SHARING KNOWLEDGE FREELY BENEFITS EVERYONE

My background in WordPress development introduced me to open source philosophy: collaboration over competition, transparency over gatekeeping, community over individual gain. I apply those same principles to UV face painting. Share techniques. Teach workshops. Answer questions. Build collective knowledge. The open source movement proved that you can give value away and still thrive. That trust in abundance over scarcity shapes how I move through creative communities.

## Transformative festival moments

Specific experiences at specific festivals that cracked open new creative possibilities.

### Park gathering, Berlin

July 2019

The first time I held a UV paintbrush

A friend handed me paints at a small outdoor gathering. I'd never painted a face before. No training, no plan, just intuition and blacklight. The person in front of me became a collaborator, not a canvas. We co-created something neither of us expected. That spontaneity, that trust in the process — it became my creative method. I've never taken a formal makeup class since. Everything I know came from that same approach: experiment, iterate, trust.

### Origin Festival, South Africa

Multiple years

Painting 20+ faces in a single night under the stars

Origin taught me stamina. A line of people waiting for UV paint, hours passing, hands steady, focus unbroken. I entered flow state so deep that time dissolved. The music, the night, the faces — all one continuous present moment. That endurance became essential. Festival painting isn't a quick gig. It's marathon creativity. Origin proved I could sustain it.

### Full Moon Party, Koh Phangan

Multiple visits

Realizing UV paint glows differently on different skin tones

Thailand's diverse crowd (locals, travelers, expats) taught me that UV pigments interact with melanin in beautiful ways. What glows electric on pale skin glows warm on darker skin. That's not a limitation — it's an opportunity. I started adapting color choices and techniques for different skin tones. Inclusion isn't just philosophical. It's technical. That awareness now guides every product choice and technique I teach.

### Vortex Festival, South Africa

Annual

Sunrise set painted under blacklight until dawn

Vortex's sunrise sets are legendary. Painting faces as the sky lightens, watching UV glow fade as natural light takes over, feeling the collective exhaustion and euphoria — these moments taught me that the work isn't about permanence. It's about presence. The makeup washes off. The photographs capture fragments. But the experience — the shared moment of creation — that lasts. Process over product, always.

### Griessmuehle, Berlin

Multiple nights

Painting in the smoky darkness of a techno club

Painting UV faces in a dark, loud, sweaty techno club is nothing like painting outdoors at a psytrance festival. Limited space. Constant movement. Bass so loud it vibrates your chest. You adapt. Faster strokes. Simpler designs. More trust in the person not to move. Griessmuehle taught me that context shapes technique. The same skills apply differently in different environments.

## Philosophical and spiritual influences

The thinkers, teachers, and traditions that shaped the worldview behind the work.

### Terence McKenna

Psychedelic philosopher and advocate for expanded consciousness

McKenna's articulation of the 'felt presence of immediate experience' and his advocacy for exploring consciousness through plant medicine opened doors of perception I didn't know existed. His talks on the importance of direct experience over received wisdom validated my intuitive, experiential approach to learning art. I don't follow formulas. I experiment, fail, learn, iterate — exactly the process McKenna championed.

### Alan Watts

Philosopher bridging Eastern wisdom and Western thought

Watts taught me that you can't grasp water by holding it tight. Creativity works the same way. The more you chase perfection, the more it eludes you. The moment you relax into process, magic happens. His concept of 'wu wei' (effortless action) describes the flow state I enter while painting. Not forcing, just allowing.

### Ram Dass

Spiritual teacher and consciousness explorer

Ram Dass's 'Be Here Now' philosophy is the mantra I repeat while painting. Not thinking about the next face in line, not worried about how the photo will look — just present with this person, this moment, this brushstroke. His teaching that 'we're all just walking each other home' reframes every interaction. The person in my chair isn't a client. They're a fellow traveler.

### Buddhist philosophy

Impermanence, non-attachment, and mindfulness

Buddhism's core teaching of impermanence is the reality of UV face painting. The work is temporary. The paint washes off. The moment passes. That impermanence isn't tragic — it's liberating. It removes pressure. It demands presence. I'm not building a legacy. I'm participating in a moment. Non-attachment to outcome allows full engagement with process.

### Joseph Campbell

Mythologist and advocate of 'follow your bliss'

Campbell's 'Hero's Journey' framework helped me understand my own story — leaving corporate comfort, entering the unknown, facing challenges, returning transformed. His advice to 'follow your bliss' validated choosing creativity over security. The UV painting isn't just a hobby. It's the call to adventure I chose to answer.

## Influence is conversation, not replication

None of these influences are blueprints to copy. They're sparks that ignited something already inside me. Android Jones didn't make me paint geometry — he showed me geometry could be sacred. Berlin techno didn't make me love bass music — it gave context to why I already did. Terence McKenna didn't make me curious about consciousness — he articulated what I'd always sensed. Influence is recognition, not imitation. These forces didn't create me. They helped me recognize what was already there.
